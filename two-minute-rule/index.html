What is the two-minute rule for habits?

The two-minute rule, popularised by James Clear, says that when you start a new habit, it should take less than two minutes to do. "Read before bed" becomes "read one page." "Do yoga" becomes "take out my yoga mat." The point isn't the two minutes of work, it's that you can't build a habit you never start, so you shrink it until starting is effortless. Once showing up is automatic, you can extend it. The rule is one of the most reliable tactics for beginners because it removes the excuse of "I don't have time."

The rule comes from James Clear's writing on using the two-minute rule to stop procrastinating and Atomic Habits. It attacks the hardest part of any habit, the first repetition on a low-motivation day.

How to shrink a habit to two minutes

Full habit → its two-minute version
The habit you wantIts two-minute version
Read 30 minutes a nightRead one page
Run three times a weekPut on my running shoes and step outside
Do a full yoga sessionTake out the mat and do one stretch
Journal every eveningWrite one sentence
Study a language dailyOpen the app and do one card
Eat healthierPut one vegetable on my plate
Meditate for 20 minutesTake three slow breaths
Do the dishes every nightWash one plate

Why it works

A habit forms through repetition in a consistent context, and repetition only happens if you actually start. By making the entry point trivial, the two-minute rule maximises the number of repetitions, which is what drives automaticity in the Lally et al. (2010) data. It also exploits a quirk of motivation: starting is the hardest part, and once you've laced up your shoes, continuing is easy. Most days "one page" turns into a chapter, but on your worst day, one page still counts, which protects the streak.

The trap: don't secretly expect more

The rule only works if two minutes genuinely counts as success. If you tell yourself "one page" but feel like you failed unless you read for thirty minutes, you've rebuilt the pressure you were trying to escape. Log the two-minute version as a win. Pair it with never miss twice so an off day doesn't spiral, and with habit stacking so the tiny habit has a reliable cue.

Frequently asked questions

What is the two-minute rule?

It's a habit-building tactic from James Clear: when starting a new habit, scale it down so it takes less than two minutes. 'Do 30 minutes of yoga' becomes 'take out my mat.' The goal is to make starting so easy you can't say no, because you can't build a habit you never begin.

Why does the two-minute rule work?

Habits form through repeated action in a consistent context, and repetition only happens if you start. Shrinking the habit removes the friction and excuses, maximising repetitions, which is what drives a behaviour toward automaticity. Starting is the hardest part; once begun, continuing is easy.

Isn't two minutes too little to make a difference?

The two minutes isn't meant to produce the result on its own, it's meant to establish the habit of showing up. Once showing up is automatic, you extend the duration. On high-motivation days you'll naturally do more; on bad days, the two-minute version still keeps the habit alive.
