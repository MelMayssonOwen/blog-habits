What is habit stacking, and how do you do it?

Habit stacking is a technique popularised by James Clear in Atomic Habits for building a new habit by attaching it to one you already do automatically. The formula is: "After [CURRENT HABIT], I will [NEW HABIT]." For example, "After I pour my morning coffee, I will meditate for one minute." It works because your existing habit is already a reliable cue, so you borrow its trigger instead of relying on memory or motivation. It's a specific, everyday version of what psychologists call an implementation intention.

The full method comes from James Clear's habit-stacking guide, which builds on a large body of research on implementation intentions — "if-then" plans that specify exactly when and where you'll act. Gollwitzer's work (1999, American Psychologist) found that people who form these concrete plans follow through far more often than those who merely intend to act.

The formula, and why each part matters

"After [CURRENT HABIT], I will [NEW HABIT]."
  • The anchor must be rock-solid. Pick a current habit you do without fail and at a consistent time — brushing teeth, starting the kettle, sitting down at your desk. Clear notes that your brain has already strengthened those neural pathways, so they make ideal cues.
  • Be absurdly specific. "After I close my laptop at the end of the workday" beats "during the afternoon." Vague cues don't fire.
  • Match the frequency. Anchor a daily habit to a daily trigger. Don't stack "review my weekly budget" onto "make coffee" if you make coffee every day.
  • Keep the new habit small. Especially at first — pair the two-minute rule with stacking so the new habit is easy enough to always complete.

How habit trackers help with stacking

A tracker's job here is to remind you of the stack at the anchor moment and to record the completion. Patterns builds the if-then structure directly into how you create a habit, so the plan and the log are the same object. But you can implement stacking in any app — Streaks, Loop, Habitify — simply by naming the habit after its cue ("After coffee: meditate") and setting the reminder to the anchor time.

Frequently asked questions

What is the habit stacking formula?

"After [CURRENT HABIT], I will [NEW HABIT]." You attach a new behaviour to an existing, automatic habit that acts as its cue, for example, 'After I pour my morning coffee, I will meditate for one minute.' The method comes from James Clear's Atomic Habits and builds on research into implementation intentions.

Does habit stacking actually work?

It's an applied form of implementation intentions, which have strong research support. Gollwitzer's 1999 work in American Psychologist found that specifying exactly when and where you'll act sharply increases follow-through versus a vague intention. Stacking works best when the anchor habit is truly automatic and the new habit is small.

How many habits can I stack together?

Start with one new habit per anchor. You can chain several habits into a routine once each link is reliable, but stacking three new behaviours at once usually fails because none of them is yet automatic. Add the next link only after the current one sticks.

What makes a good anchor habit?

Something you already do every day, at a consistent time, without having to think about it, brushing your teeth, starting the kettle, sitting down at your desk. Avoid vague anchors like 'in the morning'; specify the exact action that triggers the new habit.
