How to Build a Habit of Eating Five Portions of Fruit and Vegetables a Day

Published 2026-11-01

TL;DR: To build a habit of eating five portions of fruit and vegetables a day, link fruit and veg to things you already do. The NHS says a portion is 80g and that your five portions should be varied, while NIH guidance on implementation intentions says if-then plans help turn good intentions into action. Start with one cue, repeat it at predictable meals and snacks, and reset at the next opportunity if you miss one.

Introduction

“Five portions a day” means five portions of fruit and vegetables in total, not five of each. The NHS says a portion is 80g, and it also says those portions should come from a variety of fruit and vegetables. That variety point matters, because different fruit and vegetables contain different combinations of fibre, vitamins, minerals, and other nutrients.

The good news, if you want the low-drama version of this, is that you do not need to reinvent your entire diet. The goal here is to build one repeatable habit that makes fruit and vegetables show up in your day more reliably.

Start with one habit, not five

The easiest way to stick with a new behavior is usually not raw willpower, that famously flaky office intern. The NIH National Cancer Institute describes implementation intentions as if-then plans that link a situational cue with a response, which helps people turn goal intentions into action.

So instead of saying, “I should eat more fruit and vegetables,” make a simple plan like:

  • If I make breakfast, then I add fruit.
  • If I pack lunch, then I include a vegetable.
  • If I want a snack, then I choose a fruit or vegetable option first.

The point is to connect the habit to something that already happens, not to rely on a vague promise floating in space.

Pick your best trigger moments

Think about the parts of the day that already have structure. Breakfast, lunch, snacks, dinner, and packing food for later are all natural cue points. Those are the moments when an if-then plan is easiest to notice and use.

A few useful triggers:

  • Breakfast, when you are already handling food.
  • Lunch, when you are deciding what goes into the meal.
  • Snack time, when you might otherwise grab whatever is closest.
  • Dinner, when you can add a vegetable alongside the main part of the meal.
  • Packing food for later, when tomorrow-you will be grateful and slightly less chaotic.

Keep it flexible. You are not building a military schedule. You are creating a reliable cue so fruit and vegetables stop depending on mood, memory, or the planet aligning.

Use a worked example

Here is one realistic day built around ordinary cues and easy swaps.

Breakfast cue

You make breakfast as usual. Because you already decided on an if-then plan, you add fruit without negotiating with yourself. Maybe that means fruit with cereal, fruit with yogurt, or fruit on the side.

Lunch cue

When you pack or choose lunch, you include a vegetable. That might be a salad, sliced veg, or a sandwich that actually contains something green instead of just optimism.

Snack cue

Mid-afternoon hits and you want something quick. Because snacks are one of your trigger moments, you reach for fruit or a vegetable option first.

Dinner cue

At dinner, you add a vegetable as the default side or ingredient. You are not building a masterpiece, just making the habit happen while the meal is already happening.

Food-for-later cue

When you pack food for later, you add one fruit or vegetable item then. That way you are not trying to solve future hunger with future-you’s nonexistent focus.

This is what a habit chain looks like in practice. One cue leads to one action, and the action becomes easier to repeat because it is attached to something you already do.

Make the habit easier to repeat

A few practical supports help the habit survive contact with real life:

  • Keep visible options ready, so the easy choice is actually visible.
  • Pair fruit and vegetables with foods you already like, instead of forcing weird noble suffering.
  • Decide in advance what you will do when the usual cue is missed.
  • Make the default response simple enough that it still works when you are busy.

This is also where having a separate guide to implementation intentions can help. If you want the mechanics of if-then planning in more detail, the article on how implementation intentions help you stick to a new habit explains the cue-plus-response idea in more depth.

What to do when the day goes off track

You do not need to start over because you missed a cue. Just use the next meal or snack as your restart point.

That is the whole trick. Miss breakfast? Add fruit at lunch. Skip the planned snack? Put the vegetable in at dinner. The habit continues from the next opportunity, not from some imaginary perfect day that never existed.

This matters because the goal is consistency, not purity. One missed cue is just one missed cue.

Keep it flexible and varied

The NHS says your five portions should include a variety of fruit and vegetables. That makes the habit more workable, because you are not locked into one heroic choice forever.

So rotate your options over time. Change the fruit you keep around. Swap the vegetables in your lunches and dinners. Try different combinations until the pattern feels normal enough to repeat without much thinking.

FAQ

What counts as a portion?

The NHS says a portion of fruit or vegetables is 80g.

Do fresh, frozen, canned, dried, and juiced options all count?

Yes. The NHS says almost all fruit and vegetables count, and that they can be fresh, frozen, canned, dried, or juiced.

Do potatoes count?

No. The NHS says potatoes, yams, and cassava do not count because they mainly contribute starch to the diet.
