Habit-tracking glossary: the key terms defined

A plain-English glossary of the terms used across habit-tracking and behaviour-change research — from automaticity and cue to habit stacking, implementation intentions, and the 21-day myth. Each definition links to the science or the source where relevant. Use it as a reference alongside the tracker comparison and the research pillar.

Habit and behaviour-change terms
TermDefinition
AutomaticityThe degree to which a behaviour happens without conscious intention or effort. It's the endpoint of habit formation: when a cue triggers the action on its own. In the Lally et al. (2010) study, automaticity was measured daily and rose along a curve that plateaued after a median of 66 days.
Cue (trigger)The signal that prompts a habit — a time, place, preceding action, emotional state, or other person. Habits are cue-driven; a habit with no reliable cue depends on memory and usually fails. Habit stacking works by borrowing the cue of an existing habit.
Habit loopA common model describing habits as cue → routine → reward. The cue triggers the behaviour, the routine is the behaviour itself, and the reward reinforces it so the loop repeats.
Habit stackingBuilding a new habit by attaching it to an existing one, using the formula 'After [current habit], I will [new habit].' Popularised by James Clear; a practical form of an implementation intention.
Implementation intentionAn 'if-then' plan that specifies exactly when, where and how you'll act (e.g. 'After I brush my teeth, I will floss one tooth'). Research by Gollwitzer (1999) found these plans sharply increase follow-through versus vague goals.
Never miss twiceJames Clear's rule that missing a habit once is fine, but you should never miss two days in a row. It reframes perfectionism into resilience and prevents a single slip from becoming an abandoned habit.
Self-monitoringThe act of tracking your own behaviour or progress toward a goal. A 2016 meta-analysis (Harkin et al.) found self-monitoring promotes goal attainment, with stronger effects when progress is physically recorded.
StreakA count of consecutive days a habit has been completed. Motivating for some, but classic streaks that reset to zero on a single miss can trigger the 'what the hell' effect and cause quitting — which forgiving-streak designs try to prevent.
Two-minute ruleJames Clear's tactic of scaling a new habit down so it takes under two minutes to start ('read one page' instead of 'read for 30 minutes'), because you can't build a habit you never begin.
Forgiving streakA streak design that doesn't reset after a single missed day — for example, breaking only after two consecutive misses (Patterns) or using a gradual 'habit strength' score that recovers over time (Loop). Built to counter perfectionism.
21-day mythThe mistaken belief that habits form in 21 days, traceable to Maxwell Maltz's 1960 book Psycho-Cybernetics — an anecdotal observation about adjustment, not a habit study. Real-world data (Lally 2010) puts the median nearer 66 days.
Context-dependent repetitionRepeating a behaviour in a consistent setting so that the setting itself becomes the trigger. Identified as the core mechanism of habit formation (Gardner, Lally & Wardle, 2012).

For the studies behind these terms, see how long habits take, whether trackers work, and the primary sources below.

Frequently asked questions

What is automaticity in habit formation?

Automaticity is how much a behaviour happens without conscious effort, the endpoint of forming a habit, where a cue triggers the action on its own. In the Lally et al. (2010) study it rose along a curve that plateaued after a median of 66 days.

What is the difference between a habit stack and an implementation intention?

They're closely related. An implementation intention is any 'if-then' plan specifying when and where you'll act. Habit stacking is a specific type where the 'if' is completing an existing habit: 'After [current habit], I will [new habit].' Stacking borrows an existing routine as the cue.
