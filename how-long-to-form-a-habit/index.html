How long does it take to form a habit?

It takes far longer than the popular "21 days." In the best real-world study, Lally et al. (2010), published in the European Journal of Social Psychology, it took a median of 66 days for a new behaviour to become automatic, with a wide range of 18 to 254 days depending on the habit and the person. Simpler habits (drinking a glass of water) automated faster than harder ones (doing 50 sit-ups). The "21 days" figure is a myth that traces back to a 1960 self-help book, not a habit study.

Where "66 days" comes from

Researchers at University College London followed volunteers who chose a new eating, drinking or activity habit and did it daily in the same context, rating how automatic it felt each day. Automaticity rose along a curve that flattened over time. The median time to reach the plateau, the point where the behaviour felt automatic, was 66 days, but individuals ranged from 18 days to 254 days.

18, 254range of days to automaticity across individuals in the Lally study; the median was 66. The variation depends heavily on habit difficulty and personal consistency. Source.

Why the "21 days" myth is wrong

The 21-day claim comes from Psycho-Cybernetics (1960), in which plastic surgeon Maxwell Maltz observed that patients took "a minimum of about 21 days" to adjust to a change like a new face or an amputation. That was an anecdotal observation about psychological adjustment, not an experiment on habit formation, and "a minimum of about 21 days" quietly became "habits take 21 days" as the idea spread. There is no rigorous study supporting a fixed 21-day timeline.

What actually affects the timeline

  • Habit difficulty. Easy habits automate fastest; effortful ones take much longer.
  • Consistency of context. Doing the habit at the same time and place each day speeds automaticity, the whole mechanism runs on a stable cue (see Gardner, Lally & Wardle, 2012).
  • Missed days. Occasional misses don't reset the clock. In the Lally data a single miss didn't derail progress, see never miss twice.

What this means for using a habit tracker

Set your expectations for months, not weeks. A tracker's job is to keep you consistent through that long middle stretch where the habit isn't automatic yet and motivation dips. Judge success by whether you're showing up, not by whether it feels effortless, that feeling is the finish line, and it arrives around two months in for a typical habit, later for a hard one. Whatever tracker you choose, plan to use it well past the three-week mark.

Frequently asked questions

How long does it really take to form a habit?

In the Lally et al. (2010) study, it took a median of 66 days for a behaviour to become automatic, with individuals ranging from 18 to 254 days. The exact time depends on how hard the habit is and how consistently you do it. Plan for months, not the mythical 21 days.

Is the 21-day rule true?

No. The 21-day figure comes from Maxwell Maltz's 1960 book Psycho-Cybernetics, an anecdotal observation about patients adjusting to change, not a study of habit formation. The best evidence (Lally 2010) puts the median closer to 66 days, with a wide individual range.

Does missing a day reset the habit-formation clock?

No. In the Lally study, missing a single opportunity to perform the habit did not meaningfully affect the path to automaticity. Consistency over the long run matters far more than any single day, which is the basis of the 'never miss twice' rule.

Why do some habits take longer than others to form?

Difficulty and context. Simple habits (like drinking a glass of water after breakfast) automated much faster than demanding ones (like doing 50 sit-ups) in the research. Performing the habit at a consistent time and place also speeds up how quickly it becomes automatic.
